About the Research

Computational Analysis of Ancient Cuneiform Records

◆

This project presents a computational analysis of cuneiform administrative tablets drawn from the Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative (CDLI) dataset — the most comprehensive digital archive of ancient Mesopotamian texts in existence.

The Ur III period (c. 2112–2004 BCE) generated an extraordinary volume of bureaucratic documentation: ration lists, grain ledgers, labor allocations, and provincial tax records. By applying natural-language and quantitative methods to these texts, we can reconstruct how a pre-monetary economy managed the flow of resources across an empire.

The tablets span sites including Puzriš-Dagān, Umma, and Girsu, each reflecting a distinct node in the royal redistribution network.

~100K Cuneiform tablets in CDLI corpus
~30K Ur III administrative documents
21 Years of the Ur III dynasty (Šulgi's peak)
4,000+ Years since the last tablet was inscribed
Key Themes

What the Tablets Reveal

◆

Three interconnected themes emerge from the administrative record, each illuminating a different facet of how the Third Dynasty of Ur organized its world.

01 🌾

Grain Distribution & Ration Systems

Grain was the empire's currency of labor. Thousands of tablets record barley rations (še) allocated by gender, age, and occupation — from weavers and millers to soldiers and temple personnel. Computational analysis reveals how ration scales encoded social hierarchy across different provinces and institutional households.

Subsistence Economics
02 🏛️

Provincial Administration & the Bala System

The bala (rotation) system required each province to supply goods to the central royal storehouse at Puzriš-Dagān on a rotating schedule. Analysis of these inter-provincial flows exposes which regions were net donors, which were net recipients, and how the center maintained control over periphery.

Political Geography
03 ⚖️

Command Economy vs. Local Autonomy

How much did local administrators actually follow royal directives? Comparing tablet-level allocations against expected norms reveals systematic deviations — signs of negotiation, fraud, or informal markets operating beneath the official record. The tension between central mandates and local agency shapes the entire administrative corpus.
